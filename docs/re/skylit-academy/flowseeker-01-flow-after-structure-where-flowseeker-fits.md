## Course description

Flowseeker shows every options trade as it prints. Read the chart first, then the Heatseeker™ map, and only then weigh the flow against both. A print tells you what traded — not who, and not why.

## Section 1: Chapter Objective

You already know how a Skylit trader builds a read: the chart forms the thesis, and Heatseeker confirms or challenges it.

Flowseeker adds a third layer — **what traders actually did**, trade by trade.

This chapter teaches four things:

1. What options flow is
2. Why flow comes **after** the chart and the map
3. Why flow is not the same thing as the map
4. What a single print can — and cannot — tell you

> ***A large print earns investigation. It does not identify a profitable trade.***

## Section 2: What Options Flow Is

Every time an options contract changes hands, the trade is recorded: which contract, how many, at what price, at what moment.

That record is a **print**. The stream of prints across the market is **options flow**.

In Flowseeker, the Live Feed shows that stream — one row per print, newest at the top:

![The Flowseeker Live Feed — one row per print](https://mintcdn.com/skylit-490c28ef/QU4Pk6SK-6-klbCr/images/flowseeker-live-feed.png?fit=max&auto=format&n=QU4Pk6SK-6-klbCr&q=85&s=a5dd3405ffd82f037b5f33ad1482d8c8)

Flow is a record of the past. It tells you a trade happened. On its own, it does not tell you who made it, why, or whether they were right.

### The X-ray analogy, extended

In Charts First, the chart is the doctor's examination and Heatseeker is the X-ray.

Flow is **what the patient tells you**.

A patient's account is specific and often useful — but a good doctor does not diagnose from it alone. Patients misremember and describe symptoms that fit more than one cause. The account gets weighed against the examination and the X-ray.

Flow is treated the same way: useful evidence, checked against the structure before it earns weight.

## Section 3: Charts, Then the Map, Then Flow

| Step | Layer | The question |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Charts** | Where is price in the structure — at an extreme, or in the middle? |
| **2** | **Heatseeker** | Does dealer positioning confirm or challenge that thesis? |
| **3** | **Flow** | What are traders doing — and does it fit, or challenge, the first two? |

Flow is not where the read starts. Here is why.

Take one print: **a large call, filled at the ask.**

| Where it prints | What it means |
|---|---|
| At a **range low**, on a heavy node acting as a floor | It lines up with the structure — possible confluence |
| At a **range high**, under a heavy node acting as a ceiling | Someone betting against the structure — or hedging, or trading one leg of something larger |

Same print. Opposite meaning. The only difference is the structure around it — and without the chart and the map, you cannot see it.

The same logic covers midpoints. **We trade extremes. We avoid midpoints.** A large print in the middle of a range does not create an edge that the structure doesn't have.

> **Charts form the thesis. Heatseeker confirms or challenges it. Flow is evidence — tested against both.**

## Section 4: Flow Is Not the Map

Flowseeker and Heatseeker both come from the options market, so they are easy to blur together.

| | Flowseeker | Heatseeker |
|---|---|---|
| **Shows** | Individual trades | Modeled dealer exposure by strike |
| **Unit** | A print | A node |
| **Answers** | *What just traded?* | *Where is dealer positioning concentrated?* |

**The map is not order flow.** It models the exposure dealers carry from everything that traded before. You cannot read buyers and sellers off it, and node color does not mean "buyers here."

The two are connected: enough trading at a strike changes dealer exposure there, which is one of the ways the map **reshuffles**. But one print is not the map.

## Section 5: What a Print Can and Cannot Tell You

| A print **can** tell you | A print **cannot** tell you |
|---|---|
| Which contract traded | **Who** traded it |
| How many contracts, and the premium | **Why** |
| The exact time | Whether it **opened** or **closed** a position |
| Where it filled against the bid and ask | Whether it was **hedged** elsewhere |
| | Whether the trader will be **right** |

That right-hand column is why flow needs the chart and the map around it.

### Write the fact before the story

Traders say things like *"someone is loading up on calls."* That sounds like a description. It is actually three guesses: that it was a **buyer**, that they were **opening**, and that it was **one** person. None of those is visible in a print.

So Skylit traders write the **observation** first:

> *"2,000 SPY 590 calls — 11:14 ET — $620K premium — filled at the ask."*

Then, separately, the **interpretation**:

> *"Possibly new bullish positioning — needs the chart and the map to say whether it fits."*

Keeping the two apart is what lets you check your read later.

## Section 6: Case Study Example

*Hypothetical — constructed for teaching, not a historical trade.*

A trader sees a **$1.4M** QQQ call print, filled at the ask. They buy calls immediately. Price turns lower within the hour.

Replay it the Skylit way.

**1. Chart structure.** QQQ is at the **range high**, an area that has rejected price twice this week. An extreme — a reaction is more likely than a clean break.

**2. Heatseeker.** A heavy node sits just above price, acting as a ceiling, with little below until the midpoint. The map challenges an upside thesis.

**3. Flow.** *Now* the print: a large call into a range high, beneath a ceiling. It could be a bet against the structure, a hedge, or one leg of a spread. It is not, on its own, evidence of a breakout.

**Confluence:** none. The chart and the map point one way; one print points the other.

**Invalidation for anyone considering the upside:** acceptance above the ceiling. Until then, the print has not earned more weight than the structure.

**Decision:** no trade.

The print was the same in both versions. What changed was the order the trader looked at the evidence.

## Section 7: Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1 — Opening Flowseeker first

Flow is the most exciting screen on the platform, which is why it tempts traders to start there. Start with the chart.

### Mistake 2 — Treating a print as a signal

A print tells you something traded. It does not tell you to trade.

### Mistake 3 — Writing the story instead of the fact

"Whales are loading calls" is a guess. "Large ask-side call prints" is the observation. Write the observation first.

## Section 8: Key Takeaways

- Flow is a record of trades — what the patient tells you.
- **Charts, then Heatseeker, then flow.** The same print can mean opposite things.
- Flow shows **trades**; the map shows **dealer exposure**.
- A print shows *what* traded — not *who*, *why*, or whether it opened or closed.
- Write the fact before the story.

***A large print earns investigation. It does not identify a profitable trade.***
