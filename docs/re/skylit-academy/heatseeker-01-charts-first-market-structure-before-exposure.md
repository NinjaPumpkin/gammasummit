# Chapter Objective

Before a trader ever looks at a Heatseeker™ map, they must first understand **how to read price structure on a chart**.

Heatseeker is not a signal generator.

Heatseeker is a **confirmation tool**.

**_Charts create the initial thesis_**

-   Hitting higher timeframe resistance = bearish thesis
    
    -   Heatseeker will confirm or deny that thesis.
        

Without chart structure, exposure data becomes noise.

This chapter establishes the **Charts First Doctrine**, which will remain a core principle throughout Skylit Academy.

## Section 2: Why Market Structure Matters

Markets move within structure.

Price does not move randomly between numbers. Instead, it moves between **areas of interest**, often referred to as:

- support
- resistance

![image.png](https://d95fp8dfaxyli.cloudfront.net/academy/ch1-image.png)

- higher highs / lows
- lower highs / lows

![image.png](https://d95fp8dfaxyli.cloudfront.net/academy/ch1-image-1.png)

These structural zones represent areas where market participants previously agreed on value.

When price revisits these areas, reactions frequently occur.

Understanding these structural zones is the foundation of trading.

Before any exposure analysis occurs, traders must answer one simple question:

**Where is price located within the market structure?**

Is price:

- approaching support?
- approaching resistance?
- sitting at the middle of a range?
- trending up/down?

Each of these situations carries a very different probability of reaction - that is the essence of context.

## Section 4: Drawing Support and Resistance (The Basics)

Before we go further, let's make sure the foundation is solid.

If you're going to form a thesis from charts, you need to know how to **identify the levels that matter**.

---

## What Are Support and Resistance?

**Support** is a price zone where buyers have previously stepped in and pushed price higher. It's a floor — not a guarantee, but an area where demand has shown up before.

![image.png](https://d95fp8dfaxyli.cloudfront.net/academy/ch1-image-2.png)

---

**Resistance** is a price zone where sellers have previously stepped in and pushed price lower. It's a ceiling — an area where supply has shown up before.

![image.png](https://d95fp8dfaxyli.cloudfront.net/academy/ch1-image-3.png)

These are not magic lines. They are **areas of memory** — places where the market reacted in the past, making them likely candidates for future reactions.

---

## How to Identify Key Levels

### 1. Look for Swing Points

A **swing high** is a peak where price reversed lower. A **swing low** is a trough where price reversed higher.

![image.png](https://d95fp8dfaxyli.cloudfront.net/academy/ch1-image-4.png)

These are your building blocks. Mark the ones that are **obvious** — if you have to squint to see it, it probably doesn't matter.

### 2. Prefer Fresh Levels

A level that has not been tapped yet is stronger than one that has been tested multiple times. Each time price touches a level, it **degrades** — the reaction becomes weaker because the orders sitting there get absorbed.

Fresh levels carry the most potential energy. That's where the strongest reactions tend to happen.

[charting CH1 vid.mov](https://d95fp8dfaxyli.cloudfront.net/academy/ch1-charting_ch1_vid.mov)

---

**Exceptions:**

- **Double bottoms** — a second tap at the same low can produce a strong reversal. This is the one scenario where a re-test is worth playing.
- **S/R flips** — when a former resistance level becomes support (or vice versa), the level takes on new meaning. It's essentially fresh in its new role.

### 3. Use Higher Timeframes First

A support level on the daily or hourly chart is more significant than one on the 5-minute chart. Start with the bigger picture, then zoom in.

Think of it like this:

- **Daily/weekly levels** = major highways
- **Hourly levels** = local roads
- **5-minute levels** = side streets

You want to know where the highways are before worrying about side streets.

### 4. Recent Levels Matter More

A support level from last week is more relevant than one from six months ago. Markets evolve — the more recent the reaction, the more likely participants still care about that area.

---

## What Makes a Level "Good"?

A strong level typically has:

- **Freshness** — it has not been tapped yet, meaning the orders sitting there are still intact
- **Visible reactions** — price clearly bounced or rejected at this zone when it was first created
- **Multiple timeframe alignment** — the level shows up on more than one timeframe
- **Clean price action around it** — sharp reversals carry more weight than slow, grinding reactions
- **Magnitude of the reversal** — levels created by large, dramatic reversals carry more weight than those formed by shallow ones, because a bigger reaction signals stronger conviction from market participants.

A weak level typically has:

- Been tapped multiple times (degraded)
- Only visible on a very low timeframe
- Choppy, unclear reactions around it

---

## Keep It Simple

New traders tend to draw too many lines. The chart ends up looking like a spider web, and every level feels important — which means none of them are.

Stick to the **obvious** levels. If a level jumps out at you on a clean chart, it's probably worth marking. If you have to convince yourself it's there, skip it.

> The best levels are the ones you can spot in under five seconds.
>

## Range-Bound Action

One of the most important ideas for Skylit traders is understanding **range extremes**.

Markets makers often move within ranges.

A range consists of three primary zones:

1. Range High
2. Range Low
3. Midpoint

Range highs and lows represent **extremes**.

The midpoint represents equilibrium.

Skylit traders must focus on the extremes because they offer the best **risk-to-reward opportunities**.

![image.png](https://d95fp8dfaxyli.cloudfront.net/academy/ch1-image-5.png)

***The midpoint, by contrast, is often where price behaves unpredictably.***

This is why a fundamental Skylit principle is:

> We trade extremes.
> 
> 
> We avoid midpoints.
> 

When price is sitting in the middle of a range, there is no structural edge.

Without an edge, the trade should not exist.

## Section 5: Forming a Chart-Based Thesis

Before consulting Heatseeker, traders should develop a **chart-based hypothesis**.

Ask the following questions:

- Is price approaching support?
- Is price approaching resistance?
- Is price at the midpoint of a range?
- Is the market trending or consolidating?

These questions establish the **structural context** of the market.

# Common Mistakes

Many traders misuse exposure tools because they skip chart analysis.

***Here are three common mistakes:*** 

---

### Mistake 1 — Looking at Heatseeker Before Charts

Heatmaps are powerful, but they must be interpreted within context.

Without chart structure, traders may misinterpret exposure zones.

- Its generally not good practice to buy puts at a double bottom on higher timeframe support!

---

### Mistake 2 — Trading Midpoints

Midpoints often produce choppy price action.

They rarely offer strong asymmetric opportunities.

*This is why Skylit traders prioritize extremes.*

---

### Mistake 3 — Confusing Momentum With Structure

Just because price is moving quickly does not mean a trade exists.

Structure determines opportunity, not speed.

We can determine direction, but it wont matter unless we have a solid entry.

# Key Takeaways

- Charts provide the initial trading thesis.
- Heatseeker confirms or challenges that thesis.
- Markets move between structural zones.
- Range extremes provide opportunity.
- Midpoints often produce noise.

***Remember:***

***Heatseeker does not replace chart reading.***
